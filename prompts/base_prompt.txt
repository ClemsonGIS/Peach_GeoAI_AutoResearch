## THE TASK AND DATASET
- Single-class object detection: peaches in a commercial peach orchard in
  South Carolina, USA.
- Imagery: nadir (straight-down) drone photographs captured from approximately
  15 meters above ground level.
- The peaches are small relative to the image frame. Each peach is either
  fully visible or partially occluded by leaves and branches.
- Peaches appear in two contexts: attached to trees within the canopy, and
  fallen on the ground (grass/soil background).
- Because the view is nadir, the scene has no canonical "up" direction —
  the imagery is statistically symmetric under rotation and both horizontal
  and vertical flips.
- The dataset is split into 3 stratified, randomized folds. For each trial,
  the model is trained and validated on every fold combination and the
  reported score is the AVERAGE mAP50-95 across the 3 folds. This averaging
  reduces (but does not eliminate) evaluation noise — treat small score
  differences between trials with appropriate skepticism.

## DATASET STATISTICS
- 1,781 images total (fold 1: 586, fold 2: 598, fold 3: 597).
- 5% of images are background-only (contain no peach labels).
- Images are 256x256 pixel TILES cut from larger drone orthomosaic imagery.
  Because of tiling, some peaches are truncated at tile edges (partial
  objects with clipped bounding boxes are a normal part of the training
  and validation distribution).
- Training and inference use imgsz=640, so each 256x256 tile is UPSCALED
  2.5x before entering the network.
- Measured bounding-box statistics across the full labeled dataset
  (2,697 peach instances):
  - Instances per labeled image: median 1, mean 1.63, max 11 — scenes are
    sparse, most tiles contain a single peach.
  - Box size, sqrt(area), at NATIVE 256px tile resolution:
    p5=4.9, p25=8.6, median=12.4, p75=17.3, p95=25.5 pixels.
  - Box size, sqrt(area), at the 640 TRAINING resolution (after 2.5x
    upscale): p5=12, p25=22, median=31, p75=43, p95=64 pixels
    (absolute min ~2 px, max ~125 px).
  - By COCO size convention at training resolution: 52% of peaches are
    "small" objects (<32 px), 48% are "medium", essentially none are
    "large". The median peach sits exactly on the small/medium boundary.
  - 21% of all boxes touch a tile edge — truncated peaches are a routine
    part of both training and validation distributions due to tiling.
- Occlusion is pervasive: a large fraction of peaches are partially hidden
  or cut off by foliage, so the visible evidence for many objects is a
  small colored fragment rather than a complete round fruit. Detection
  therefore depends heavily on local color/texture cues surviving
  augmentation, and labels frequently cover partially invisible extents.
- This is a small dataset by object-detection standards, and the model is
  the smallest (nano) variant — factor both into how much regularization
  via augmentation is likely to help versus how much label/object
  corruption the small objects can tolerate.

## THE MODEL
- Ultralytics YOLO26 nano: an NMS-free, end-to-end detector. Its training
  recipe includes Progressive Loss (ProgLoss) and Small-Target-Aware Label
  assignment (STAL), which guarantees positive label assignments for small
  objects. It removes Distribution Focal Loss and uses a lightweight
  regression head. This architecture is explicitly designed to improve
  small-object detection relative to earlier YOLO versions, which is
  relevant to how aggressively augmentation can shrink or corrupt small
  objects before recall degrades.
- The nano variant has limited capacity. Consider what regularization
  pressure a small model needs versus a large one when reasoning about
  augmentation strength.

## TUNABLE HYPERPARAMETERS (name: type, allowed range — Ultralytics semantics)
- hsv_h: float, 0.0 to 0.1 — random hue shift as a fraction of the hue wheel.
- hsv_s: float, 0.0 to 0.9 — random saturation jitter fraction.
- hsv_v: float, 0.0 to 0.9 — random brightness/value jitter fraction.
- degrees: float, 0.0 to 45.0 — max random rotation in degrees.
- translate: float, 0.0 to 0.9 — max random translation as fraction of image size.
- scale: float, 0.0 to 0.95 — random scale gain (zoom in/out range).
- shear: float, 0.0 to 10.0 — max shear angle in degrees.
- perspective: float, 0.0 to 0.001 — random perspective warp coefficient.
- flipud: float, 0.0 to 1.0 — probability of vertical flip.
- fliplr: float, 0.0 to 1.0 — probability of horizontal flip.
- bgr: float, 0.0 to 1.0 — probability of swapping RGB channels to BGR.
- mosaic: float, 0.0 to 1.0 — probability of 4-image mosaic augmentation.
- mixup: float, 0.0 to 1.0 — probability of mixup (blending two images and labels).
- cutmix: float, 0.0 to 1.0 — probability of cutmix (pasting a region from another image).
- copy_paste: float, 0.0 to 1.0 — probability of copy-paste augmentation
  (copying object instances between images).
- close_mosaic: int, 0 to 10 — number of FINAL epochs during which mosaic is
  disabled so the model fine-tunes on full-resolution, unfragmented images.

## HOW TO REASON (guidance, not answers)
Think mechanistically about how each augmentation interacts with THIS data:
- Photometric augmentations (hsv_h, hsv_s, hsv_v) trade off illumination
  robustness against corruption of the color cues that distinguish peaches
  from surrounding green foliage. Consider which channel of variation
  (hue vs. saturation vs. brightness) naturally varies in outdoor drone
  imagery versus which carries the class-discriminative signal. Consider
  also that channel-swapping (bgr) is a categorical color change, not a
  jitter.
- Geometric augmentations (degrees, flipud, fliplr, translate, shear,
  perspective) should be evaluated against the true invariances of nadir
  imagery. Ask: which transformations produce images that could plausibly
  occur in the real test distribution, and which produce off-distribution
  distortions? Consider that geometric warps also distort the axis-aligned
  bounding boxes of small round objects.
- Scale and mosaic-family augmentations (scale, mosaic, mixup, cutmix,
  copy_paste, close_mosaic) interact with object size. Half the peaches
  are already COCO-"small" at training resolution, with a p5 around 12
  pixels — and mosaic additionally downsizes each source image when
  compositing four tiles into one canvas, while scale-down zooming shrinks
  objects further still. Augmentations that shrink, blend, or fragment
  objects can push a meaningful fraction of the distribution below the
  detectable size or corrupt their labels, hurting small-object recall —
  but the same augmentations are also the strongest regularizers and
  context-diversifiers for a dataset of this size and a nano model, and
  scenes are sparse (median 1 instance per tile), which mosaic partially
  compensates for by increasing instances per training canvas. Weigh
  these tensions explicitly. Note that copy_paste may have limited or no
  effect when only bounding-box (not segmentation) labels are available.
- The tiling process already produces edge-truncated objects — 21% of all
  boxes touch a tile edge — and the validation set contains them too.
  Consider which augmentations (translate, mosaic) mimic this truncation
  phenomenon versus which introduce distortions never seen at validation
  time.
- Interactions matter: total augmentation strength is roughly the
  composition of all enabled augmentations, so evaluate the combined
  policy, not each knob in isolation.
